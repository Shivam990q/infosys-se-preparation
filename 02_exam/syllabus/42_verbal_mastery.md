## 2. The 12 Golden Rules of Error Detection & Sentence Correction

### Rule 1: Intervening Phrases & Subject Isolation
Phrases enclosed between commas or starting with prepositions like *as well as, along with, together with, in addition to, including, accompanied by, accompanied with* do **NOT** change the grammatical number of the subject:
* **Incorrect:** The Chief Minister, along with his cabinet ministers, are visiting the flood-affected areas.
* **Correct:** The Chief Minister, along with his cabinet ministers, **is** visiting the flood-affected areas.
*(Subject is "Chief Minister" [Singular]; "cabinet ministers" is part of the parenthetical prepositional phrase).*

---

### Rule 2: Correlative Proximity Rule
When two subjects are joined by *neither...nor, either...or, not only...but also, or, nor*, the verb must agree in number and person with the **CLOSER SUBJECT**:
* **Incorrect:** Neither the manager nor the software developers was aware of the database breach.
* **Correct:** Neither the manager nor the software developers **were** aware of the database breach.
*(The closer subject is "software developers" [Plural], so the verb must be plural).*

---

### Rule 3: Indefinite Pronoun Singularity
Words such as *each, every, everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, no one, nobody, anyone, anybody, neither of, either of, one of* are grammatically **SINGULAR** and demand singular verbs and pronouns:
* **Incorrect:** Each of the engineering trainees have submitted their weekly project report.
* **Correct:** Each of the engineering trainees **has** submitted **his or her** weekly project report.
* **Special Trap — "One of the [Plural Noun] Who [Verb]":**
  * When modified by a relative pronoun (*who, which, that*), the verb agrees with the plural noun immediately preceding it:
  * "He is one of the **engineers who have** designed the distributed cache." *(Plural)*
  * "He is the **only one** of the engineers **who has** designed the distributed cache." *(Singular)*

---

### Rule 4: Dangling & Misplaced Modifiers
An introductory participial phrase (-ing or -ed phrase) must logically and grammatically modify the **SUBJECT IMMEDIATELY FOLLOWING THE COMMA**:
* **Incorrect:** Walking through the Mysore campus at night, the magnificent library building looked breathtaking.
  *(This implies the library building was walking!)*
* **Correct:** Walking through the Mysore campus at night, **I thought** the magnificent library building looked breathtaking.
* **Correct Alternative:** As I walked through the Mysore campus at night, the magnificent library building looked breathtaking.

---

### Rule 5: Parallelism in Correlatives & Series
Items in a list or connected by conjunctions (*not only...but also, both...and, either...or, than, as*) must be in the **IDENTICAL GRAMMATICAL STRUCTURE**:
* **Incorrect:** Shivam likes coding microservices, to debug complex algorithms, and reading tech papers.
* **Correct:** Shivam likes **coding** microservices, **debugging** complex algorithms, and **reading** tech papers.
* **Correlative Parallelism:**
  * **Incorrect:** She not only excels in Java but also in Python.
  * **Correct:** She excels **not only in Java but also in Python**. *(Both follow the preposition 'in').*

---

### Rule 6: Pronoun Case & Antecedent Alignment
* **Subjective Case (*I, he, she, we, they, who*):** Used as subjects of verbs or predicate nominatives following forms of "to be":
  * "It was **he** who wrote the clean code" *(Not "him")*.
* **Objective Case (*me, him, her, us, them, whom*):** Used as direct/indirect objects or objects of prepositions:
  * "Between you and **me**" *(Not "between you and I")*.
* **Comparison Case:**
  * "He can code faster than **I** [can code]" *(Not "than me")*.

---

### Rule 7: Tense Sequencing & The Past Perfect Trap
Do not use the Past Perfect (*had + V3*) in isolation. It is reserved strictly for the **EARLIER OF TWO PAST ACTIONS**:
* **Incorrect:** I had reached the office yesterday.
* **Correct:** I **reached** the office yesterday.
* **Correct Past Perfect Usage:** When the manager arrived (Action 2: Simple Past), the developers **had already deployed** the patch (Action 1: Past Perfect).

---

### Rule 8: The Three Conditional Sentences
| Conditional Type | "If" Clause (Condition) | Main Clause (Result) | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Type 1 (Real/Likely)** | Simple Present (`V1`) | `will / can / may + V1` | If you **study** daily, you **will clear** Infosys SE. |
| **Type 2 (Hypothetical)** | Simple Past (`V2 / were`) | `would / could + V1` | If I **were** the team lead, I **would automate** tests. |
| **Type 3 (Impossible Past)** | Past Perfect (`had + V3`) | `would have + V3` | If you **had practiced** PYQs, you **would have solved** it. |

---

### Rule 9: Redundancy & Tautology Eradication
Infosys sentences frequently test identifying redundant combinations:
* ❌ *Revert back* $\rightarrow$ ✅ **Revert**
* ❌ *Return back* $\rightarrow$ ✅ **Return**
* ❌ *Consensus of opinion* $\rightarrow$ ✅ **Consensus**
* ❌ *Reason is because* $\rightarrow$ ✅ **Reason is that**
* ❌ *Sufficient enough* $\rightarrow$ ✅ **Sufficient** (or Enough)
* ❌ *Repeat again* $\rightarrow$ ✅ **Repeat**

---

### Rule 10: Negative Inversion & Conjunction Pairing
When negative adverbs (*hardly, scarcely, seldom, rarely, barely, no sooner, neither*) start a sentence, **SUBJECT-AUXILIARY INVERSION** is mandatory:
* **"Hardly / Scarcely" Pair with "WHEN":**
  $$\text{Hardly } + \text{had } + \text{Subject } + V_3 \dots \text{ WHEN } + \text{Simple Past}$$
  *Example:* "Hardly **had I started** the exam **when** the timer began ticking." *(Not "than").*
* **"No sooner" Pairs with "THAN":**
  $$\text{No sooner } + \text{had / did } + \text{Subject } + V_3 / V_1 \dots \text{ THAN } + \text{Simple Past}$$
  *Example:* "No sooner **had the candidate entered** the room **than** the interviewer asked about OOP."

---

### Rule 11: Prepositional Collocations (Fixed Prepositions)
| Incorrect Form | Correct Collocation | Meaning / Usage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Different than | **Different from** | Contrast comparison |
| Comply to | **Comply with** | Adhere to rules/regulations |
| Insist to | **Insist on (+ V-ing)** | Demand persistently |
| Prohibit to enter | **Prohibit from entering** | Forbid an action |
| Refrain to | **Refrain from (+ V-ing)** | Abstain consciously |
| Congratulate for | **Congratulate on** | Praise for an achievement |
| Discuss about | **Discuss [No preposition]** | Deliberate on a topic |

---

### Rule 12: Subjunctive Mood (Desires & Requirements)
Verbs expressing recommendation, demand, urgency, or hypothetical wishes take the base verb (*be / V1 without -s*):
* **Demand / Insist:** "The lead insisted that the engineer **submit** [not *submits*] the pull request immediately."
* **Hypothetical Wishes:** "I wish I **were** [not *was*] in Mysore."

## 3. Reading Comprehension (RC) — 3-Minute Strategic Execution

Infosys gives 1 passage with 4–5 questions. Never read the entire passage word-by-word first.

### The 4-Step Skimming Architecture:
1. **Step 1: Read the First and Last Sentence of Every Paragraph (45 Seconds)**
   * First sentence gives the claim/topic.
   * Last sentence provides the transition or conclusion.
2. **Step 2: Read Question Stems First (30 Seconds)**
   * Identify question types: Factual vs Inferential vs Tone.
   * Note down distinctive anchor keywords (e.g., *carbon sequestration, Quantum computing, 1998 Treaty*).
3. **Step 3: Targeted Scanning (45 Seconds)**
   * Scan the text specifically for the anchor keywords.
   * Read 1 sentence before and 1 sentence after the keyword match.
4. **Step 4: Answer Elimination (60 Seconds)**
   * Eliminate options with extreme words (*always, never, completely, universally, all*).
   * Prefer balanced, moderate statements (*often, can lead to, tends to, partially*).

## 4. Para Jumbles (Sentence Rearrangement) Decryption Matrix

### Mandatory Pair Detection Techniques:
1. **Acronym & Full Name Rule:**
   * Sentence introducing the full name ("Tata Consultancy Services (TCS)") ALWAYS precedes the sentence using the abbreviation ("TCS").
2. **Noun-to-Pronoun Sequencing:**
   * A sentence with a specific noun ("The Infosys Foundation") precedes sentences using pronouns ("It", "They", "The organization").
3. **Chronological Markers:**
   * Look for time markers: *Initially $\rightarrow$ Subsequently $\rightarrow$ Eventually $\rightarrow$ Finally*.
4. **Contrast & Transition Markers:**
   * Words like *However, But, In contrast, On the other hand, Nevertheless* signal that this sentence must immediately follow a contrary statement.

## 5. Real Solved Infosys Verbal Questions

### Question 1: Dangling Modifier Error Detection
**Sentence:**
*"Having spent weeks reviewing the system architecture documentation, the database migration was executed without a single glitch by the DevOps engineer."*

**Error Analysis:**
* The introductory participial phrase is *"Having spent weeks reviewing the system architecture documentation"*.
* Grammatically, this must modify the subject following the comma.
* In the sentence, the subject after the comma is *"the database migration"*.
* A database migration cannot spend weeks reviewing documentation!
* **Correction:** *"Having spent weeks reviewing the system architecture documentation, **the DevOps engineer executed** the database migration without a single glitch."*

---

### Question 2: Parallelism with Correlatives
**Sentence:**
*"The software architect neither approved of the microservices design nor the proposed monolithic database schema."*

**Error Analysis:**
* In *neither...nor*, what follows *neither* must grammatically match what follows *nor*.
* Here, *neither* is followed by a prepositional phrase *"of the microservices design"*, while *nor* is followed by a noun phrase *"the proposed monolithic database schema"*.
* **Correction:** *"The software architect approved of **neither the microservices design nor the proposed monolithic database schema**."*
